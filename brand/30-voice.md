# Voice

The register is a records office, not a brokerage. Certain about what it has seen, explicit about what it has not, and completely uninterested in persuading anyone.

## Four principles, worked

### 1 · State the record, not the conclusion

> **No.** Harper & Row owns the film rights.
> **Yes.** Recorded transfer to Harper & Row, `1989-04`. No later transfer or termination recorded. Tier 1.

The second is longer and it is the product. The first is the thing VI forbids.

### 2 · Dates beat adjectives

> **No.** A recent deal suggests the rights were optioned.
> **Yes.** Deal announcement `2009-06`, no production credit since. Tier 3, as of `2026-09`, decaying.

*Recent* was true when someone wrote it and is false now. The as-of is what keeps the sentence honest a year from now.

### 3 · Uncertainty is a number

> **No.** We think this is probably still with the author, though it's hard to say.
> **Yes.** `NEVER·GRANTED` — tier 4. No recorded grant, no adaptation, no deal trace, author living. Confirm with a targeted records search on the work and party.

Tier 4 is not an apology. Per VII it is the cheapest deal type in the corpus, and the tone should carry that: this is a promising lead stated at its true strength.

### 4 · Name the next step

Every uncertain statement ends in a `confirming_action`. Not *we could look into it* — the specific action, its cost and its latency, from the ladder in VII.

> Tier 3 → tier 2: the publisher's permissions desk confirms who controls dramatic rights. A phone call, a few days.

## The first-contact letter

The single most consequential piece of writing in the business, and the hardest. It has to carry two disclosures from VI — take your own legal advice, and we may want to acquire — and still be the reason somebody calls back.

What makes it work is that the disclosures come *first* and are stated without apology. A letter that buries them reads as a pitch that got caught. A letter that opens with them reads as a party that expects to be checked.

**Shape:**

1. Who we are and what we do, in two sentences, with no adjectives.
2. What the public record shows about this specific work, with dates and the sources named.
3. The disclosure, plainly: we may be interested in acquiring adaptation rights, and for that reason you should take independent legal advice before acting on anything here. We cannot advise you.
4. What we are actually asking for — usually one factual question, not a meeting.
5. How to make us go away. A single line, honoured.

**Never in this letter:** a deadline, a valuation, a computed termination date presented as an opportunity, any sentence beginning *you could*, or any statement about what the recipient should do.

The letter is set in `serif`. Computed values inside it — the publication date, the registration number — set in `mono`, which is the one place the house type rule does real work in front of a stranger: it visibly separates what we looked up from what we are saying.

## Register by audience

| Audience | Register |
| --- | --- |
| The operator | Terse. The queue is a worklist; every line answers *what do I do next with this book*. No encouragement, no summary statistics presented as progress. |
| A producer or business-affairs reader | Evidentiary. They are checking whether this survives their own diligence, so lead with the tier and the source, not the recommendation. |
| An author, illustrator, heir or estate | Plain, short, and disclosed. Assume the letter will be read aloud to a lawyer, and write it so that reading goes well for us. |
| Counsel | Cite the statute and the date math, show the rule version, and stop. Never characterise what the law means for their client. |
